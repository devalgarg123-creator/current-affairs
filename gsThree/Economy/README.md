1) Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 2026
The Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 2026, came into force on 1 October 2026, replacing the colonial-era Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 1891.

The new legislation updates the legal framework governing the use of banking records as evidence in judicial proceedings.

Background: Banks increasingly maintain records electronically rather than exclusively in physical registers.

These records include:
Digital transaction histories.
Electronic account statements.
Cloud-based banking records.
Electronic backups.
Digital banking documents.

The earlier legislation was enacted in a period when banking records were predominantly maintained in physical form.

Major features of the new legislation
A. Recognition of digital records: The new law recognises banking records maintained in electronic, digital, virtual and cloud-based formats.

B. Technology neutrality: The framework accommodates different methods of storing and maintaining banking records.

C. Certification of records: The legislation provides for certification of physical and electronic records, subject to prescribed requirements.

D. Data integrity: Electronic records must meet the applicable requirements for authenticity and protection against unauthorised alteration.

E. Court proceedings: The legislation provides safeguards concerning the summoning of bank officials when banks are not parties to a case.

Significance
Digital transformation of banking
The law recognises the changing nature of banking and the increasing use of digital records.
Judicial efficiency
A clear framework for admitting banking records as evidence can facilitate financial litigation.

Challenges
Cybersecurity: Digital records may be vulnerable to unauthorised access
Data integrity: Courts must be able to assess the authenticity of records
Technical capacity: Judicial institutions need the capacity to handle electronic evidence
Privacy	Banking: records contain sensitive financial information
Interoperability: Different banking systems may use different formats

Way forward
Establish robust digital record management systems.
Strengthen cybersecurity and access controls.
Ensure standardised certification procedures.
Train judicial and banking personnel in digital evidence.
Maintain appropriate safeguards for customer privacy.

Mains question
The digitisation of banking requires corresponding reforms in the legal framework governing financial records. Discuss with reference to the Bankers' Books Evidence Act, 2026.

2) GST 2.0 — Proposed decriminalisation of GST offences
The proposed GST 2.0 reforms may remove GST-specific arrest powers, with the issue expected to be discussed at the 57th GST Council meeting on 7 October 2026. Vajiram & Ravi
Current system
Under Section 69 of the CGST Act, the Commissioner can authorise arrest for specified serious GST offences when there are legally sufficient grounds.
Examples include:
- Fake invoices
- Fraudulent ITC
- Invoices without actual supply
- Collecting GST but not depositing it
- Wrongful ITC claims
Between 2021–22 and 2024–25, central GST formations reportedly made 887 arrests in 72,393 GST-offence cases. Vajiram & Ravi
Why decriminalise?
Arguments in favour:
- Reduce fear of tax administration
- Improve ease of doing business
- Increase taxpayer confidence
- Separate genuine disputes from deliberate fraud
- Reduce administrative overreach
But the counterargument
If enforcement becomes too weak:
Fraud → revenue loss → lower compliance
Therefore:
Routine/default tax disputes can be treated administratively, while deliberate fraud should continue to attract strong criminal enforcement.

GST reform: https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2320934&lang=1&reg=48
Benefits: Lower compliance costs, improved cash flow, fewer disputes and reduced fear of disproportionate enforcement.
Concerns: Enforcement must remain effective against deliberate fraud, fake invoices and fraudulent ITC claims. Excessive relaxation could weaken deterrence.

3) RBI — Financial stability and emerging risks
RBI Governor Sanjay Malhotra highlighted the need to remain vigilant about financial stability, warning that prolonged stability itself can encourage excessive risk-taking and leverage. 

What is financial stability?
The ability of the financial system to:
Absorb shocks while continuing to provide essential financial services.

It includes Banks, NBFCs, Financial markets, Payment systems, Financial infrastructure

RBI's five broad priorities
1. Systemic resilience: Financial institutions should withstand shocks.
2. Identify emerging systemic risks
Risks may come from:
- Geopolitical conflict
- Cyberattacks
- Technology failures
- Supply-chain disruption
- Climate change
- Financial-market shocks
3. Better granular data
Regulators need detailed information about:
- NBFC exposures
- Interconnected institutions
- Cross-border positions
- Technology dependencies
4. System-wide resilience
Consider: Banks + NBFCs + payment systems + markets + technology infrastructure
5. Innovation with trust
AI, tokenisation and new financial technologies can improve efficiency but can also create new systemic risks.
Important current data:
Net FDI during the first four months of 2026–27 stood at around $13.43 billion, reported as 38% higher than the corresponding period of the previous year.
At the same time, foreign investors reportedly sold around $10.35 billion of Indian stocks and bonds in 2026–27.

Mains line
Financial stability increasingly requires monitoring the entire financial ecosystem rather than treating banks as isolated institutions.

4) 1. ₹10,000 crore SME Growth Fund
The Union Cabinet approved a ₹10,000 crore commitment towards SME Growth Fund (SGF).

The fund is intended to provide long-term equity capital to high-potential Small and Medium Enterprises, especially businesses that are ready to scale up. It will operate through an Alternative Investment Fund (AIF) structure.
problem: Micro enterprises → relatively more access to small-ticket support
but Growing SMEs → difficulty obtaining patient growth equity. This creates a "missing middle" in business finance.
The new fund tries to address this.

Priority areas include Manufacturing, Technology, Innovation, Strategic value chains, Industrial clusters,Tier-II and Tier-III cities

The government expects it to help firms Expand manufacturing capacity, Adopt technology, Enter global value chains, Increase exports, Create employment

Important data
According to the government, MSMEs account for approximately:
- 31.1% of GDP
- 35.4% of manufacturing output
- 48.58% of exports

Therefore, equity can be particularly useful when a growing firm needs capital to scale but does not want excessive leverage.

7) RBI monetary policy: Repo rate at 5.50%
The RBI raised the repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% on 7 October 2026, its first increase in nearly four years, amid inflation concerns linked to higher oil prices and weaker monsoon conditions. The policy stance also shifted from neutral to calibrated tightening. 
Static concepts
- Repo rate — rate at which the RBI lends short-term funds to banks against eligible securities.
- Monetary transmission — how policy-rate changes affect lending rates, consumption, investment and inflation.
- Inflation targeting — monetary policy framework centred on price stability while keeping growth in mind.
Possible effects
Higher repo rate → costlier borrowing → moderation in credit demand → potential easing of inflationary pressure.
But higher rates can also raise borrowing costs for businesses and households and potentially moderate investment.
Important analytical point: Monetary tightening can manage demand-side inflation, but it cannot directly solve supply shortages caused by oil disruptions or deficient agricultural output.
Mains-ready line:
Effective inflation management requires monetary policy to be complemented by supply-side measures, energy diversification and resilient agricultural supply chains.

8) Retail inflation rises to 5.54%
India's retail inflation rose to 5.54% in September 2026, compared with 4.83% in August. Food inflation increased to 7.74% from 5.97%. These are provisional year-on-year figures.

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level of goods and services. It reduces the purchasing power of money when income does not rise at the same rate.
Types to know 
- Demand-pull inflation: demand grows faster than supply.
- Cost-push inflation: input costs, such as fuel or transport, rise.
- Food inflation: prices of food items rise.
- Core inflation: a commonly used measure excluding volatile food and fuel components; exact definitions can vary by analytical context.

Why does rising inflation matter?
1. Households: Food-price increases disproportionately affect low-income families because food takes up a larger share of their expenditure.
2. Monetary policy: Persistent inflation can constrain the Reserve Bank of India's room to reduce policy rates.
3. Businesses: Higher input costs can reduce profit margins or raise selling prices.
4. Agriculture: Food inflation may reflect supply shortages, weather disruptions or distribution inefficiencies.
5. Growth: High and persistent inflation creates uncertainty for consumption and investment.

What should policymakers do?
- Improve storage, cold chains and agricultural logistics.
- Reduce avoidable supply-chain bottlenecks.
- Use calibrated food-stock and trade measures where appropriate.
- Coordinate fiscal and monetary policy without undermining price stability.
- Protect vulnerable households through targeted support.
A supply-driven food-price shock cannot always be resolved by higher interest rates alone. Monetary policy affects aggregate demand, while food supply requires agricultural, logistical and fiscal interventions.
